1. Introduction and Purpose

This Policy establishes Ai-ME BANK's ("the Bank") enterprise-wide framework for the responsible design, approval, deployment, monitoring, and retirement of Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems that act autonomously or semi-autonomously on the Bank's behalf — referred to as "AI Agents" and, where multiple such agents interact, "Agentic AI Systems."

Agentic systems differ materially from traditional predictive or generative AI: they do not merely produce an output for a human to act upon — they can independently decide and execute actions, invoke tools and APIs, delegate work to other agents, and retain context across a task. This shift from "AI that informs" to "AI that acts" is treated in this Policy as a materially higher risk class, requiring governance beyond conventional model risk management.

This Policy is built around the RBI's Framework for Responsible and Ethical Enablement of Artificial Intelligence ("FREE-AI"), released 13 August 2025, and consolidates the Bank's obligations under RBI's technology, outsourcing, lending, KYC, fraud-risk, payment-security, and cybersecurity regulations, CERT-In's cyber security directions, and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act and Rules, as detailed in Section 3.

1.1 Objectives

  • Establish clear Board-level accountability for AI Agents and Agentic AI Systems across the Bank.
  • Distinguish AI Agent Governance (controls over a single autonomous system) from Agentic AI Governance (controls over multiple interacting agents), and mandate both, calibrated to risk.
  • Classify every AI Agent by risk tier (Section 7) and autonomy level (Section 8), and ensure technical capability is never mistaken for business authority.
  • Preserve the customer's and the Bank's right to human review and override of any AI-driven decision.
  • Ensure full traceability and auditability of autonomous actions, for RBI, CERT-In, and internal/statutory audit.
  • Protect customer data consistent with the DPDP Act/Rules and RBI data localisation requirements.

1.2 Statement of Intent

This Policy favours proportionate, risk-tiered governance over blanket restriction, consistent with FREE-AI's "Innovation over Restraint" Sutra. The goal is to let AI Agents and Agentic AI Systems move at pace within clear guardrails, not to treat innovation and public trust as competing goals.

2. Scope and Applicability

2.1 Entities and Systems Covered

This Policy applies to Ai-ME BANK, its Indian branches, subsidiaries and group entities engaged in RBI-regulated activity, and all employees, contractors, and third parties (including Lending Service Providers and AI/cloud vendors) who design, build, deploy, or operate AI Agents on the Bank's behalf.

This Policy applies to any AI system that can select and execute an action without prior human approval of that specific instance, invoke external tools or APIs, delegate a task to another agent, or retain state across a task in a way that influences future autonomous decisions. Systems that only generate a recommendation for a human to act upon are governed by the Bank's Model Risk Management Policy, and by Sections 13 and 16–17 of this Policy for data and security purposes.

2.2 Effective Date and Existing Systems

This Policy applies immediately to all new initiatives. AI Agents already in production shall be inventoried (Section 10.5) and brought into compliance within [180 days] of the Effective Date, coordinated by the CAIO Office and reported to the ITSC and RMCB.

3. Regulatory Foundation

This Policy must be read together with the following instruments. The full reference table with dates is at Annexure B.

3.1 FREE-AI — Framework for Responsible and Ethical Enablement of Artificial Intelligence

Released by the RBI on 13 August 2025 (Committee chaired by Prof. Pushpak Bhattacharyya, IIT Bombay), FREE-AI sets out seven Sutras — Trust is the Foundation; People First; Innovation over Restraint; Fairness and Equity; Accountability; Understandable by Design; and Safety, Resilience and Sustainability — across six pillars (Infrastructure, Policy, Capacity, Governance, Protection, Assurance) and 26 recommendations, and expressly calls for a Board-approved AI policy with lifecycle governance. Section 5 maps each Sutra to a specific control in this Policy.

3.2 RBI (IT Governance, Risk, Controls and Assurance Practices) Directions, 2023

Issued 7 November 2023, effective 1 April 2024. Requires a Board-approved IT Governance Framework, an IT and Information Security Risk Management Framework, defined Board/ITSC/Head of IT roles, and periodic RMCB review of IT and cyber risk. This Policy operates as an AI/agent-specific extension of that framework.

3.3 RBI (Outsourcing of Information Technology Services) Directions, 2023

Issued 10 April 2023, effective 1 October 2023. Governs any AI Agent, model, or orchestration layer that is hosted or operated by a third party, including cloud providers and AI/LLM vendors. Section 14 sets out the agent-specific due diligence and contracting requirements that apply in addition to this Direction.

3.4 RBI Master Directions on Fraud Risk Management (15 July 2024)

These consolidated Directions require an Early Warning Signals (EWS) framework, Red-Flagged Account (RFA) classification, and reporting of qualifying frauds to the RBI's Central Repository of Information on Large Credits (CRILC) and Central Fraud Registry. Where an AI Agent is used in fraud detection or EWS scoring, its output is an input to this framework, not a substitute for it: closure of a fraud case or an RFA classification remains a non-delegable human decision (Section 9).

3.5 RBI KYC Directions, 2016, as amended

Governs Customer Due Diligence (CDD) and ongoing due diligence. AI Agents may assist in document verification or risk-scoring, but may never waive a KYC requirement, auto-close a KYC exception, or reset a customer's authentication factors autonomously (Section 9).

3.6 RBI (Digital Lending) Directions, 2025

Issued 8 May 2025, consolidating prior digital lending guidelines. Requires disclosure of key facts to borrowers, explicit consent for automated credit-limit increases, Lending Service Provider due diligence, and reporting of Digital Lending Apps to the RBI's Centralised Information Management System (CIMS).

3.7 RBI Digital Payment Security Controls and applicable payment directions

Cover authentication, API security, and fraud monitoring for digital payments. No AI Agent may be granted unrestricted authority over payment initiation, beneficiary addition/change, or authentication resets (Section 9).

3.8 RBI Cyber Security Framework for Banks (2 June 2016) and the ITGRCA Directions' cyber-risk provisions

Set baseline security controls, annual penetration testing, and RBI incident-reporting timelines. Section 19 extends these to AI Agent-specific incidents.

3.9 CERT-In Cyber Security Directions, 2022

Issued by India's Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) under Section 70B of the Information Technology Act, 2000, these Directions impose obligations independent of, and in addition to, the RBI's own cyber-incident reporting regime, including mandatory reporting of specified categories of cyber incidents to CERT-In within 6 hours of detection or being made aware of them, retention of ICT system logs for 180 days within India, and time synchronisation of relevant systems. Section 17 addresses how this applies specifically to AI Agent incidents — a requirement distinct from, and not satisfied merely by, RBI incident reporting.

3.10 RBI Directions on Storage of Payment System Data

Issued 6 April 2018 and subsequently clarified, requiring payment system data to be stored exclusively within India, with a narrow exception for the foreign leg of cross-border transactions. Section 13.3 addresses the specific implications for AI Agents using overseas-hosted models.

3.11 Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025

The DPDP Rules, 2025 were notified by MeitY on 13–14 November 2025, bringing the Data Protection Board into operation immediately while phasing in core obligations (consent, notice, security safeguards, breach notification, data-principal rights) over an 18-month period. AI Agents that process personal data must be designed to the standard these Rules will impose by their effective dates, not merely to the parent Act.

3.12 Banking Regulation Act, 1949 and Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934

This Policy and the Directions above are issued under powers including Section 35A of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949 and Section 45L of the RBI Act, 1934. No AI Agent may exercise a statutory or regulatory function, or take any action, that law requires to be performed by a natural person or the Board.

Regulatory hierarchy rule: where this Policy is less stringent than a binding law, RBI Direction, CERT-In Direction, or supervisory instruction, the binding requirement prevails. Where this Policy is more stringent, the Bank follows this Policy unless a documented, legally permissible exception exists (Section 24). This Policy reflects the Bank's understanding as of the version date on the cover page; Legal and Compliance must validate every citation in Annexure B against the RBI and CERT-In websites before Board adoption and at every review cycle (Section 24.1).

7. Risk Classification Framework (Tier 0–4)

Every proposed AI Agent is classified into one of five risk tiers before development begins, and reclassified whenever its scope of authority materially changes. Risk Tier answers "what is at stake"; Autonomy Level (Section 8) answers "how much freedom does the agent have" — the two are assessed together but are independent dimensions.

TierDefinitionIllustrative ExampleMinimum Governance Requirement
Tier 0 — ExperimentalNo production data; sandbox only; no customer or financial effect.Synthetic-data experiments; model benchmarking.AI Governance Council delegated technical approval.
Tier 1 — Low ImpactInternal productivity use; no material customer or financial effect.Internal drafting assistant; internal risk-summary agent; code-review agent.Business Owner + AI Governance Council approval; annual revalidation.
Tier 2 — ModerateCustomer-facing or operational decision support with reversible outcomes; human reviews before action.Customer-service triage agent; document classification; internal risk analysis.AI Governance Council + Risk/Security/Privacy review; independent validation; monthly monitoring.
Tier 3 — High ImpactMaterial effect on customers, credit, fraud, compliance, or reputation.Credit recommendation; AML alert prioritisation; fraud-detection scoring; collections strategy.Executive AI Council approval + independent validation + formal go-live sign-off.
Tier 4 — Critical / AutonomousAgent can materially alter records, initiate financial actions, or operate with high autonomy.Payment execution agent; core-system change agent; autonomous treasury action.Board / Board-designated committee approval; normally prohibited for autonomous execution (see Section 9).

7.1 Tiering Procedure

  • The Business Owner completes the Risk Tier & Autonomy Level Assessment (Annexure C) at concept stage, before build work begins.
  • The Executive AI Governance Council reviews the assessment and assigns a provisional tier and autonomy level.
  • For Tier 3, the CRO and CISO must countersign; for Tier 4, the full business case must be presented to the RMCB and approved by the Board before any pilot beyond a controlled sandbox.
  • The assigned tier and level are recorded in the AI Agent Register (Section 10.5) and revalidated at least annually.

7.2 Reclassification Triggers

  • The agent is granted access to a new data source, system, or external tool.
  • The agent's permitted autonomy is increased.
  • The agent begins delegating tasks to, or receiving delegated tasks from, another agent.
  • A Tier-relevant incident (Section 19) occurs involving the agent.

8. Agentic AI Autonomy Control Framework (A0–A5)

New in this version

Every AI Agent is assigned an explicit autonomy level in addition to its risk tier. Technical capability must never be interpreted as business authority: an agent's effective permissions are the intersection of business authorisation, system entitlements, data access, tool allowlists, and policy-engine decisions — not merely what the underlying model is technically capable of doing.

LevelPermitted PatternMandatory Controls
A0 — Observe OnlyRead or monitor systems; takes no external action.Read-only credentials; full logging of every access.
A1 — RecommendGenerates a recommendation for a human to act upon; takes no action itself.Human acceptance required; rationale and evidence recorded with every recommendation.
A2 — Execute Reversible, Low-Risk TasksCreates drafts, tickets, or summaries; performs non-material workflow steps.Tool allowlist; rate limits; rollback capability; full audit trail.
A3 — Execute Bounded Operational ActionsCompletes multi-step workflows within predefined limits.Human-on-the-loop supervision; policy-engine enforcement; transaction limits; circuit breakers; continuous monitoring.
A4 — High-Impact, Semi-AutonomousTakes high-impact actions, but only at mandatory human-approval gates.Human-in-the-loop approval at every gate; dual control for financial actions; strong authentication; immutable audit log.
A5 — Unrestricted Autonomous AuthorityNo routine production use is permitted at this level.Prohibited in production unless specifically approved by the Board with demonstrated compliance with every applicable requirement; financial authorisation always remains human-controlled regardless of this exception.

8.1 Relationship Between Risk Tier and Autonomy Level

A high Risk Tier must never be paired with a high Autonomy Level by default. As a floor: Tier 0–1 agents may operate up to A2; Tier 2 agents may operate up to A3 with Human-on-the-Loop supervision; Tier 3 agents may operate up to A3 only, and any A4 assignment for a Tier 3 agent requires Executive AI Governance Council and CRO sign-off in addition to the Tier 3 approval; Tier 4 agents may never be assigned A4 or A5 in production except through the exceptional, Board-approved, demonstrably compliant pathway described in the A5 row above, and even then, financial authorisation itself remains outside the agent's authority under Section 9.

8.2 Recording and Review

The assigned autonomy level, and the date it was last reviewed, is recorded in the AI Agent Register (Section 10.5) alongside the risk tier, and is subject to the same reclassification triggers (Section 7.2).

9. Non-Negotiable Agent Restrictions

New in this version — elevated from section on human oversight

The following restrictions apply to every AI Agent regardless of risk tier or autonomy level, and may not be waived by any exception process (Section 24). They are stated here as a standalone, auditable list precisely so that no individual approval can quietly erode them.

⛔ Absolute restrictions — no exception process may waive these
  • No agent may independently authorise a customer payment or funds transfer, change a beneficiary, reset an authentication factor, release collateral, approve a loan, increase a credit limit, waive a KYC/AML requirement, close a fraud case or Red-Flagged Account, or override any regulatory control.
  • Agents shall not be given shared human credentials, generic administrator accounts, or unrestricted production API keys; agent credentials shall be unique, scoped to minimum required permissions, short-lived where technically feasible, and continuously monitored.
  • Every externally consequential agent action shall be attributable to an agent identity, initiating context, approver identity (where applicable), tool, timestamp, data sources, and resulting system state.
  • Agents shall not alter their own policy, permissions, code, safety limits, logging configuration, or underlying model without a controlled change process (Section 15.2).
  • Agents shall not self-provision additional tools, sub-agents, credentials, or cloud resources.
  • Agent-to-agent communication shall be authenticated and logged; delegation shall never increase the effective privilege available to the receiving agent beyond what the delegating agent itself held, unless a fresh, independent approval is obtained (Section 11.1).
  • Model Context Protocol (MCP), Agent-to-Agent (A2A), or equivalent integration interfaces shall be treated as privileged integration surfaces, governed by the same API security and least-privilege standards as any other production system access (Section 16).

These restrictions are drafted deliberately as absolutes rather than tiered guidance. If a genuine business need appears to require an exception to any restriction in this section, that is a signal to escalate to the Board via Section 24, not to reinterpret the restriction.

10. AI Agent Governance — Per-Agent Controls

This section sets out mandatory controls for every individual AI Agent. Section 11 sets out additional controls that apply only where multiple agents interact.

10.1 Scope of Authority

Every AI Agent operates under a documented, approved statement of scope: the systems/data it may access, the actions it may take, any financial/transactional limits, and the specific circumstances requiring escalation to a human.

10.2 Least-Privilege Access

Each agent is granted the minimum data and system access required for its approved function. Broader access requires written justification and CISO sign-off.

10.3 Monitoring and Performance

Every agent is monitored against: task-completion rate; the ratio of autonomous-to-escalated actions (with a documented threshold for sudden shifts); accuracy/error rate; and cost per completed task, since a single task may involve multiple tool invocations.

10.4 Runtime Data Access Logging

Because an agent may decide at runtime which system or tool to call, each agent maintains a runtime log of what it actually accessed for each task, not merely what it is permitted to access, so any divergence can be detected.

10.5 AI Agent Register

The CAIO Office maintains a central register of every agent in development, pilot, or production: name and purpose; risk tier and autonomy level; Business and Technical Owner; date of last validation; accessible systems/data; and operational status. Available to Internal Audit, and to the RBI or CERT-In on request.

10.6 Talent and Ownership

Operating AI Agents responsibly requires skills distinct from conventional AI/ML model-building — agent orchestration engineering and agent-specific security testing. HR, in consultation with the CAIO, maintains a specific talent-gap assessment for these roles.

11. Agentic AI Governance — System-Level Controls

Where two or more agents interact — by delegation, joint workflow completion, or communication via protocols such as MCP or A2A — the per-agent controls in Section 10 are necessary but not sufficient. Problems can emerge purely from agent interaction even where every individual agent behaves within its own approved parameters.

11.1 Permission Inheritance Rules

Where Agent A delegates to Agent B, the Bank defines in advance, in writing, whether B inherits A's restrictions or uses its own independently approved permissions. Per the Section 9 restriction, delegation shall never increase effective privilege without a fresh, documented approval — this must never be left to default or implicit behaviour.

11.2 Agent-to-Agent Trust Boundary Map

For every Agentic AI System, the Technical Owner maintains a current diagram of which agents may call which other agents and tools, reviewed by the CISO before production and after any topology change.

11.3 System-Wide Kill-Switch

Every Agentic AI System has a single tested control capable of halting the entire multi-agent workflow, tested before go-live and at least semi-annually thereafter.

11.4 End-to-End Trace Logging and Delegation Audit Trail

A single trace log follows a task across every agent it touches. A separate delegation audit trail records which agent handed a task to which other agent, with what permissions, at what time — this is what makes the Section 11.1 inheritance rule independently verifiable.

11.5 Emergent-Behaviour Review

The Executive AI Governance Council conducts a periodic review — at minimum quarterly for Tier 3–4 Agentic AI Systems — for outcomes where every individual agent behaved within its approved parameters, but the combination produced an unintended result (e.g., two agents independently approving the same refund).

11.6 Continuous Runtime Monitoring

Because agents in an Agentic AI System act continuously, monitoring uses continuous behavioural baselines and real-time anomaly detection, not solely periodic drift checks calibrated for static models.

Sections 10 and 11 are cumulative: any Agentic AI System must satisfy every applicable Section 10 control for each constituent agent, in addition to every Section 11 control for the system as a whole.

12. Human Oversight and Escalation

12.1 Right to Human Review

Any customer materially affected by an AI Agent's action has the right to request human review. This right is disclosed to the customer at or before the action is communicated (Section 18).

12.2 Escalation Design

Every Tier 2+ AI Agent has a documented, tested escalation path defining precisely when the agent must stop and refer to a human, who that human is, and the maximum time before further escalation.

12.3 Override Logging

Every instance where a human overrides, reverses, or declines to act on an agent's proposed action is logged and reviewed periodically by the Business Owner for systematic patterns suggesting the agent needs revalidation.

The absolute restrictions on what an agent may never do autonomously — regardless of tier, level, or escalation design — are set out in Section 9, not here.

13. Data Governance, Privacy and Localization

13.1 Data Protection Principles

All personal data accessed by an AI Agent is handled per the DPDP Act/Rules and the Bank's Data Governance Policy. Purpose limitation and data minimisation are enforced through least-privilege tool scoping (Section 10.2): an agent receives access to specific fields, not entire records, wherever feasible.

13.2 Consent for Automated Decisions

Where an agent's action affects a borrower's credit limit or terms, explicit borrower consent is obtained beforehand, per the RBI (Digital Lending) Directions, 2025. Blanket, bundled, or pre-ticked consent does not satisfy this requirement.

13.3 Data Localisation

Payment system data, per RBI's Directions on Storage of Payment System Data, is stored exclusively within India. Where an agent uses an overseas-hosted model or AI/LLM provider, the Bank ensures — via contract and technical controls verified by the CISO — that no payment system data is transmitted to or retained on infrastructure outside India, other than the narrow permitted exception for the foreign leg of cross-border transactions. This is a hard constraint on vendor selection for any Tier 2+ agent touching payment data, not a negotiable preference.

13.4 Dynamic Data Access Assessments

Because an agent may decide at runtime which tool or data source to call, a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) prepared at design time cannot fully anticipate every access pattern. A DPIA is required before any Tier 2+ agent is deployed; adding a new tool or data source triggers a fresh DPIA; the runtime access log (Section 10.4) is reconciled against the DPIA's assumptions at least quarterly.

13.5 Vendor Data Use Restrictions

Contracts with AI/LLM vendors prohibit use of the Bank's customer data to train or fine-tune the vendor's models, absent a specific, time-bound, written exception approved by the Chief Data Officer and Chief Compliance Officer.

14. Third-Party, Outsourcing and Vendor AI Governance

An AI Agent's involvement does not reduce the Bank's responsibility for outcomes delivered through outsourced systems. This section applies in addition to the Bank's existing Outsourcing and Third-Party Risk Management Policy.

14.1 Due Diligence

Before onboarding any vendor whose product includes an AI Agent component, the Bank assesses the vendor's own AI governance maturity, whether its system can be risk-tiered under Section 7, its incident-notification commitments, and its ability to support the Bank's audit, logging, and kill-switch requirements.

14.2 Contractual Requirements

Contracts include, at minimum: a right to audit; the data-use restriction in Section 13.5; an obligation to notify the Bank of any security incident affecting the agent within a period at least as strict as the Bank's own regulatory reporting timelines (Sections 17, 19); a right to independently test the vendor's system for the security risks in Section 16; and exit/transition-assistance terms.

14.3 Concentration Risk

The Executive AI Governance Council monitors concentration of Tier 3+ AI Agent functionality in any single vendor or model provider, and reports material concentration to the RMCB.

14.4 Lending Service Providers

Where an LSP uses an AI Agent on the Bank's behalf, the Bank remains solely responsible for compliance with the RBI (Digital Lending) Directions, 2025, and ensures the LSP's agent is registered in the AI Agent Register (Section 10.5) notwithstanding third-party operation.

15.1 Independent Validation Standards

Performed by a function independent of the build team, per the Model Risk Management Policy, and additionally testing, for Tier 2+ agents: resistance to prompt injection; behaviour under tool-chaining attack scenarios; and, for Agentic Systems, enforceability of permission inheritance rules under adversarial conditions.

15.2 Change Management

Any material change to scope of authority, data access, underlying model, risk tier, autonomy level, or an Agentic System's topology triggers Stages 3–4 afresh, not a routine configuration change.

15.3 Model Version Change Management

A foundation-model version change (e.g. an upgrade to a newer Claude model release) is explicitly in scope of Section 15.2, even where no other property of the agent changes, and is never treated as a routine configuration update. Before a new model version is promoted to production for any Tier 1+ agent:

  • The Model Risk Management function re-runs the accuracy, bias, and security regression tests from Section 15.1 against the new version, using the same test set as the prior validation where possible, to isolate model-driven behaviour change from data drift
  • Any material change in output distribution, refusal behaviour, or tool-use pattern versus the prior version is documented and reviewed by the Executive AI Governance Council before promotion, not discovered after the fact
  • The old and new model versions are both recorded in the AI Agent Register (Section 10.5), with the promotion date, validator, and a rollback path to the prior version retained for at least one validation cycle
  • For Tier 3–4 agents, the new version runs in shadow mode alongside the incumbent for a defined period before full cutover, consistent with the Controlled Pilot stage above

16. Cybersecurity Controls for Agentic Systems

This section applies in addition to the Bank's Information Security and Cyber Security Policies, addressing attack surface a conventional static-model threat model does not cover.

16.1 Threat Modelling

Prior to deployment of any Tier 2+ agent, the CISO's function conducts an AI-specific threat model covering: prompt injection leading to real action; tool-chaining exploits; credential/permission exposure through agent logs; and, for Agentic Systems, escalation via delegation.

16.2 Minimum Security Architecture

  • A dedicated AI gateway / control plane for model access, authentication, policy enforcement, rate limiting, and telemetry — no agent connects directly to core systems or databases.
  • Agent identity integrated with enterprise IAM/PAM; no shared or generic credentials.
  • A tool registry with allowlisting, schema validation, and per-tool risk classification; tool arguments validated independently of the model, not trusted at face value.
  • A policy engine positioned between agent reasoning and tool execution, so the model can never bypass policy enforcement directly.
  • Secrets held in a vault, never placed in prompts, logs, or agent memory in plain text.
  • Immutable, tamper-evident audit logs covering prompts (where permitted), retrieved sources, tool calls, approvals, and resulting outputs.

16.2.1 Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

  • Every knowledge source has a named owner, approval status, version, and retention rule.
  • Retrieval respects the requesting user's or agent's own authorization context — an agent must never retrieve what its human counterpart could not.
  • Retrieved content is treated as data, not as a trusted instruction, unless the source is explicitly designated as trusted.
  • Stale or superseded documents are removed or clearly versioned so they cannot silently influence a live decision.

16.2.2 Tool / MCP Interfaces

  • Every tool has an owner, risk classification, schema, permission scope, and documented failure behaviour.
  • Tool servers must not expose unrestricted filesystem, shell, database, or network capability to an agent.
  • High-risk tools require an additional policy check and human approval gate, consistent with the agent's autonomy level (Section 8).

16.2.3 Agent-to-Agent (A2A) / Multi-Agent

  • Each agent has a unique identity and a defined scope of responsibility; agent messages are authenticated and logged.
  • The parent/originating agent in a delegation chain remains accountable for the delegated action within its workflow (Section 11.4).
  • The system enforces global action budgets and stop conditions across the entire agent graph, not only per-agent limits.
  • No emergent multi-agent behaviour may be cited as a justification for bypassing this Policy.

16.3 Penetration Testing

Tier 2+ agents and Agentic AI Systems are included in the Bank's annual penetration testing programme conducted by CERT-In empanelled auditors, with specific test cases drawn from Annexure F.

16.4 Business Continuity

The Bank's Business Continuity Policy is extended to cover failure or unavailability of any Tier 2+ agent, including a defined manual fallback capable of sustaining the affected business function until the agent is restored or the kill-switch condition is resolved.

17. CERT-In Compliance and Cyber Incident Reporting

New in this version

This section addresses an obligation distinct from, and in addition to, the RBI incident-reporting requirements in Section 19. Both must be satisfied in parallel where applicable; complying with one does not discharge the other.

17.1 Legal Basis

CERT-In's Cyber Security Directions, 2022, issued under Section 70B of the Information Technology Act, 2000, apply to the Bank as a body corporate operating computer resources in India, independent of its status as an RBI-Regulated Entity.

17.2 Six-Hour Reporting Obligation

Where an AI Agent Incident (Section 19.1) falls within CERT-In's specified categories of reportable cyber incidents — including, without limitation, unauthorised access to or compromise of a system, data breach or data leak, or an incident affecting critical information infrastructure — it must be reported to CERT-In within 6 hours of the Bank detecting it or being made aware of it. This timeline is materially shorter than typical internal escalation cycles and must be built into the incident response runbook (Section 19.2) as a hard deadline, not a target.

17.3 Log Retention and Time Synchronisation

The Bank shall retain ICT system logs relevant to AI Agent and Agentic AI System operation for the period mandated by CERT-In (currently 180 days), with those logs maintained within India, and shall ensure relevant systems are synchronised to the standard time source specified by CERT-In.

17.4 Applicability Judgement

Not every AI Agent Incident will meet CERT-In's specified reportable categories; determining whether a given incident qualifies is a Legal/Compliance/CISO judgement, made promptly given the 6-hour clock. Ambiguous cases should be resolved in favour of reporting rather than waiting for certainty.

17.5 Coordination with RBI Reporting

Where an incident triggers both this Section and Section 19's RBI reporting obligations, the Bank shall run both notification workstreams in parallel from the point of detection, coordinated by the CISO, rather than sequencing one after the other.

18. Customer Protection, Disclosure and Grievance Redressal

18.1 Disclosure of AI Involvement

Customers are clearly informed, in plain language, whenever an AI Agent is involved in a decision or communication materially affecting them. Dark patterns designed to obscure this disclosure are prohibited.

18.2 Right to Explanation

Where an agent's action adversely affects a customer, the customer is entitled to a plain-language explanation of the principal basis for that action, consistent with FREE-AI's "Understandable by Design" Sutra.

18.3 Grievance Redressal

Grievances relating to an AI Agent's action are handled through the Bank's existing grievance mechanism and the Reserve Bank – Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, without lower priority merely because AI was involved. AI-related grievances are specifically tagged and reported to the Executive AI Governance Council monthly.

18.4 Fair Practices

Agents engaged in customer communication, collections, or recovery operate within the Bank's Fair Practices Code and shall not employ manipulative design, undue pressure, or misleading representations.

19. Incident Management and Regulatory Reporting

19.1 AI Agent Incident Definition and Severity

An "AI Agent Incident" includes any autonomous action outside approved scope, a successful prompt-injection or tool-chaining exploit, an Agentic AI System emergent-behaviour failure, a kill-switch activation, or customer harm/financial loss from an agent's action. Severity is classified as follows:

SeverityIllustrative EventMinimum Response
SEV-1 CriticalUnauthorized financial action, major customer harm, material data breach, systemic agent runaway, critical control bypass.Immediate containment; kill-switch activation; executive escalation; Legal/Compliance assessment; RBI, CERT-In (Section 17), and customer notification as applicable.
SEV-2 HighMaterial wrong decisions, significant privacy leakage, widespread hallucination, high-impact model failure.Containment within a defined SLA; root-cause analysis; independent review; Board reporting as appropriate.
SEV-3 MediumLimited impact, repeated control violations, significant quality degradation.Remediation plan; monitoring; management reporting.
SEV-4 LowNon-material defect or isolated error without customer harm.Corrective action and trend monitoring.

19.2 Internal Escalation

AI Agent Incidents are reported immediately by the Technical Owner to the CISO and CAIO Office, logged in the Bank's standard incident system, tagged as AI Agent-related.

19.3 RBI Regulatory Reporting Timelines

Where an incident meets the criteria of a reportable cyber security incident under RBI's framework, it is reported within the prescribed timelines (in current practice, approximately 2 hours for incidents involving customer data compromise or financial loss, and approximately 6 hours for other significant incidents), followed by a detailed root-cause report (currently approximately 21 days). Legal and Compliance must confirm exact current timelines at the time of any actual incident, and must additionally assess CERT-In applicability under Section 17 in parallel, not sequentially.

19.4 Evidence to Preserve

  • Agent and user identity; prompt/request and relevant context, subject to privacy and retention rules.
  • Retrieved sources/documents and their versions; model/provider/version and configuration.
  • Tool calls, parameters, approvals, and downstream responses; policy-engine decisions and blocked actions.
  • Output presented to the customer/user and the actual downstream effect; containment, root cause, and remediation.

19.5 Root Cause Analysis

Every significant AI Agent Incident undergoes documented root cause analysis, including, for Agentic System incidents, whether it arose from a single agent's behaviour or from inter-agent interaction, with findings presented to the Executive AI Governance Council and, for Tier 3–4 agents, the RMCB.

19.6 Post-Incident Kill-Switch Review

Following any kill-switch activation, the affected system is not restored to production until effectiveness is confirmed and the root cause is remediated and independently re-validated.

20. Monitoring, Internal Audit and Assurance

20.1 Continuous Monitoring

Tier 2+ agents are subject to continuous, automated monitoring against Sections 10.3 and 11.6, with alert thresholds and named recipients defined prior to go-live.

20.2 Internal Audit Coverage

Internal Audit includes AI Agent governance in its annual audit universe, ensuring every Tier 4 agent is independently audited at least annually, with unrestricted access to the AI Agent Register, validation records, monitoring dashboards, and incident logs.

20.3 Concurrent Audit

Where concurrent audit covers a function relying on a Tier 3–4 agent for transaction processing or credit decisioning, its scope is extended to sample-test that agent's autonomous decisions against this Policy.

20.4 Assurance Reporting to the Board

A consolidated assurance report — Register summary, incident statistics, audit findings, outstanding remediation — is presented to the RMCB at least half-yearly and summarised to the Board at least annually.

20.5 Regulatory Examination Readiness

The CAIO Office ensures the Register, validation records, risk tier/autonomy assessments, and incident logs can be produced to the RBI or CERT-In within the timeframe specified for supervisory examination.

21. Training and Capacity Building

  • Mandatory annual training on this Policy for all staff who own, build, operate, or approve AI Agents, with role-specific modules for Business Owners, Technical Owners, Risk/Compliance, and Internal Audit.
  • Specialised training on agent orchestration engineering and agent-specific security testing for relevant technical staff, recognising these as distinct from general ML engineering.
  • AI Agent governance awareness in induction programmes for new employees in Technology, Risk, Compliance, and Business functions.
  • Training completion records maintained as evidence for regulatory examination.

24. Policy Review, Exceptions and Enforcement

24.1 Review Cycle

Reviewed at least annually by the CAIO Office and recommended for RMCB/Board approval, and additionally whenever: the RBI or CERT-In issues a binding regulation materially affecting AI governance; a significant AI Agent Incident indicates a control gap; or the Bank materially changes its AI risk appetite.

24.2 Exceptions

Any exception must be requested in writing, stating the specific control from which an exception is sought, the compensating control, and the time-bound period, approved by the Executive AI Governance Council (Tier 0–2) or the RMCB (Tier 3–4). 24.3 Enforcement

Non-compliance is addressed through the Bank's existing disciplinary and vendor management frameworks; material or repeated non-compliance is reported to the RMCB, Internal Audit, and the Chief Compliance Officer.

24.4 Policy Owner

The Chief AI Officer is the named owner of this Policy, responsible for its accuracy, review, and consistent application across the Bank.
